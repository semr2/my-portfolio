03 — Phishing

Phishing is a common online scam where attackers try to trick people into clicking dangerous links, opening suspicious attachments, sharing information, or taking unsafe actions.

Welcome

Phishing attacks are designed to look familiar, urgent, or important. They often pretend to be from a trusted service, a company, a bank, a school, or a delivery service. The goal is to trick a person into acting quickly without thinking.

In phishing, attackers may try to get you to click a malicious link, open a dangerous attachment, reply with private information, or provide a security code. A safe learner does not click first and does not share sensitive information in a suspicious message.

Learning goal

By the end of this module, you will be able to recognize common phishing attempts, identify warning signs, and make safer decisions before clicking, replying, or sharing information.

What Is Phishing?

Phishing is a scam that uses deceptive messages or fake websites to trick people. Attackers often impersonate a trusted sender or brand to create a false sense of urgency or trust.

🎭

Impersonation

Attackers may pretend to be a familiar organization or a person you know. The message may look official, but it is not trustworthy.

🔗

Deceptive links

Phishing messages may include links that lead to fake login pages or harmful websites designed to collect information or install malicious content.

📎

Suspicious attachments

Unexpected attachments can contain harmful files or links. Opening them without checking can create risk.

Everyday example

A message says, “Your account is locked. Reset your password now.” The message includes a link that looks official, but it actually sends you to a fake website.

This is a classic phishing pattern: urgency, pressure, impersonation, and a malicious or deceptive link.

Email Phishing

Email phishing is one of the most common forms of phishing. These messages often pretend to be account alerts, delivery notices, invoices, fake prizes, or urgent requests for action.

Email inspection example

From: security-update@account-support.example Subject: Immediate action required

Dear user,

Your account will be disabled unless you confirm your access today.

https://verify-account-now.example-login.xyz

Reply with your password and one-time code if you want to continue using the service.

Warning signs include a suspicious sender address, pressure to act quickly, a strange link, and a request for a password or verification code. A trustworthy service would not ask for these details through an unexpected email.

📬

Check the sender

Look closely at the email address. A real organization usually uses a consistent and recognizable domain.

✍️

Look at the wording

Urgent, threatening, or overly dramatic wording is a common phishing warning sign.

🧭

Verify independently

Use a trusted method, such as a bookmark or a known app, rather than clicking from the message itself.

SMS Phishing / Smishing

Phishing is not limited to email. Messages sent by SMS, WhatsApp, Telegram, or similar apps can also be scams. These messages often ask for immediate action, encourage payment, or provide suspicious links.

Example SMS

Your package has been delayed. Click now to confirm delivery: bit.ly/3x4m9k

Suspicious details include a shortened link, an urgent message about a delivery, and a request to act without verifying the sender or service.

  • Unexpected messages from unknown numbers.
  • Shortened links or unfamiliar websites.
  • Requests for payment or verification codes.
  • Urgent fake delivery, account, or prize alerts.
  • Messages that ask you to reply or click before verifying the real source.

Fake Websites

Fake websites may imitate a legitimate login page or service. They are created to trick people into entering passwords, codes, or personal information. Even a professional-looking page can be unsafe.

🌐

Check the website address

Look closely at the address bar. Fake websites often use unexpected domain names, misspellings, or unfamiliar pages.

🔖

Use trusted bookmarks

When possible, open a service through a known browser bookmark or a trusted app rather than a message link.

🔐

HTTPS is not enough by itself

HTTPS helps protect the connection, but it does not prove the website is legitimate. A fake site can still use HTTPS.

Safe practice

If you receive a message telling you to log in, stop. Open the trusted service directly through a bookmark or known app, then check whether the alert is real.

Common Warning Signs

These are common signs that a message may be phishing. Look for one or more of these signals before you click, reply, or share information.

Pressure

Urgent or threatening

Messages that say “act now” or “your account will be closed today” are often designed to create panic.

Pressure can cause people to make rushed choices without checking the message first.

Suspicious contact

Unknown sender or strange address

Unexpected senders, odd domains, or inconsistent names may indicate a scam.

Attackers often create addresses that look close to real ones but are not trustworthy.

Requests

Requests for sensitive data

Any request for passwords, MFA codes, payment details, or personal information should be treated with caution.

Legitimate organizations normally do not ask for passwords or verification codes through uncertain messages.

Links

Suspicious links

Shortened links, odd domains, or unfamiliar URLs can send you somewhere dangerous.

Malicious links may lead to fake login pages or harmful downloads.

Attachments

Unexpected attachment

Opening a file from an unexpected message can expose a device to risk.

Attachments can contain harmful content, even when the message appears convincing.

Offer

“Too good to be true”

Messages promising prizes, rewards, or quick money are often scams.

Scammers use enticing offers to draw attention away from caution and encourage action.

What Should You Do?

When you suspect a message or link is unsafe, follow a simple process.

STOP
↓
CHECK
↓
VERIFY
↓
REPORT
↓
DELETE / IGNORE
  • Stop before clicking links or opening attachments.
  • Check the sender, ask yourself if the message is expected, and look at the wording.
  • Verify unusual requests through a trusted channel, such as a known app or a direct website you already use.
  • Avoid replying to suspicious messages or sharing passwords, MFA codes, or personal data.
  • Report suspicious messages through the organization’s approved reporting process if needed.
  • Delete or ignore the message when it is clearly suspicious.

Key Takeaways

  • Think before you click.
  • Unexpected messages deserve extra attention.
  • Never share passwords or verification codes through messages.
  • Check sender information, links, and wording.
  • Verify unusual requests independently.
  • When in doubt, stop, check, and verify.
