04 — Social Engineering

Social engineering is the use of deception and manipulation to influence how people think, feel, and act online or in person. It is a human-centered attack method that often tricks people into sharing information, changing settings, or bypassing normal security steps.

Welcome

Cybersecurity is not only about technology. People are often the first target in an attack. Attackers may try to manipulate someone into revealing information, clicking a dangerous link, changing a setting, transferring money, or ignoring a security process.

The best defense is simple: STOP, THINK, and VERIFY before acting. A safe response is based on evidence, not pressure.

Core idea

STOP → THINK → VERIFY

When a message creates pressure, urgency, fear, or excitement, pause and confirm before acting.

What Is Social Engineering?

Social engineering is the use of deception or psychological manipulation to influence a person’s behavior. It is designed to make someone trust a request, act quickly, or ignore normal safety steps.

👑

Authority

Someone claims to be in charge, senior, or official, and expects the learner to follow without question.

⏱️

Urgency

Someone says the request must be handled immediately, which can make people rush instead of verifying.

😟

Fear

Messages may try to create worry or panic so the person acts before thinking clearly.

🎁

Reward

Attackers may promise a prize, benefit, or shortcut to make someone act without checking.

🤝

Helpfulness

Someone may appear helpful or friendly, encouraging a quick action that breaks normal rules.

🕵️

Curiosity

Attackers may create a sense of interest or mystery to push a person to click or investigate.

Impersonation

Impersonation is when someone pretends to be a person, team, or organization that seems trustworthy. A person claiming to have authority does not automatically make the request legitimate.

Fictional example

A person calls and says, “I am from the IT office. I need your password to fix your account right away.”

A safe response is to ask for a trusted contact method, verify the identity independently, and never share credentials because someone sounds official.

  • Someone may claim to be an IT employee, manager, support person, or delivery agent.
  • Authority can be faked or mistaken.
  • Trust must be confirmed, not assumed.
  • Safe communication uses a known or approved method.

Urgency and Pressure

Attackers often create urgency to discourage careful thinking. The goal is to make the person act before verifying. Urgency is a reason to slow down, not to skip checks.

  • “Act immediately.”
  • “Your account will be disabled.”
  • “This must be done right now.”
  • “Do not tell anyone.”
  • “You have only a few minutes.”

Safe response

When a message creates pressure, stop and verify the request before taking any action.

Manipulation and Emotional Pressure

Attackers may target emotions such as fear, excitement, curiosity, sympathy, embarrassment, or pressure to help. They want the victim to act emotionally instead of thoughtfully.

Choose the emotional pressure used in each example.

Example 1: “If you do not help now, the issue will be worse tomorrow.”

Example 2: “This is a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity. You must act before anyone else does.”

Verification

Verification is the process of checking whether a request is real before you respond. An unusual request should always be tested against trusted procedures and methods.

ACT SAFELY
  • Stop before taking the requested action.
  • Check exactly what is being requested.
  • Verify the person’s identity through a trusted communication method.
  • Use the organization’s approved security procedures.
  • Follow your organization’s approved reporting procedure if something seems suspicious.

Safe Response Checklist

  • [ ] Stop
  • [ ] Think
  • [ ] Check the request
  • [ ] Verify identity
  • [ ] Use a trusted communication channel
  • [ ] Follow security procedures
  • [ ] Report suspicious activity if appropriate

When something feels unusual or pressured, slow down and verify before acting.

Key Takeaways

  • Authority can be faked.
  • Urgency should not override security procedures.
  • Emotional pressure can influence decisions.
  • Never skip verification because someone sounds convincing.
  • Verify unusual requests independently.
  • Do not share sensitive information just because someone asks.
  • When in doubt, stop and verify.
